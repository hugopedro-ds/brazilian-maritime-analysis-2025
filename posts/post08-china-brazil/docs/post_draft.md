## POST TEXT (English) — FINAL

Brazil-China is Brazil's largest bilateral trade lane. But the structure of that lane is much more asymmetric than the headline number suggests.

Using ANTAQ + ComexStat 2025 data, maritime mode only:
– 96% of Brazil's exports to China are raw commodities: soybeans, iron ore, oil and sugar. Finished goods represent only 0.3%.
– 65% of China's exports to Brazil are finished goods: machinery, electronics, vehicles and industrial products. Raw goods represent only 6%.
– Average value per tonne: USD 645 leaving Brazil. USD 1,953 entering Brazil.

That asymmetry also appears in the ports.

Chinese exports to Brazil move mainly through container hubs. Santos alone handles around 40% of those flows, and the top 5 ports account for 79%.
Brazilian exports to China follow a different logic. They move largely through specialised bulk and commodity terminals. Itaguaí and São Luís handle 28% combined as Vale's iron-ore terminals; Campos dos Goytacazes ships 7% in offshore oil from the Bacia de Campos. These ports are often connected to dedicated logistics systems and specialised operators.

Different cargo. Different infrastructure. Different market dynamics.

For procurement teams and freight forwarders modelling this corridor, the key point is simple.

Brazil-China is not one market. It is two structurally different markets sharing the same flag-pair.

The front-haul, China → Brazil, behaves like a container market, with port concentration and pricing power dynamics.
The back-haul, Brazil → China, behaves more like a commodity market, routed through specialised terminals and dedicated export corridors.

What you save — or fail to save — on this corridor depends on which side of the lane you are buying and which terminal you are routed through.

When you model your China-Brazil rates, are you treating the lane as one market, or two?
