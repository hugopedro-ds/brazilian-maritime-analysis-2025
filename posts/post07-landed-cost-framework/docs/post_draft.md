I spent several years working in port operations across Africa — Luanda, Maputo, Beira and Nacala. In many of those markets, carrier choice at terminal level was not really a choice. Most ports had a single container terminal; Luanda was the exception, but its terminals were operated by carrier-affiliated companies. Either way, you picked a terminal and the terminal decided which carriers you got. The alignment was structural, not a market.

Brazil looks different on the surface — multiple terminals, multiple carriers, more visible optionality. But there is also a layer Angola and Mozambique did not have: state-level tax regimes. Santa Catarina, Espírito Santo and others have built decades of import incentives around their ports — a fiscal layer pure logistics analysis misses.

So in Brazil, the "best" port is not always the closest, the fastest, or the one with the widest carrier choice. Sometimes it is simply the port that makes the tax math work. That is not wrong — it often makes perfect commercial sense. But there is a trade-off that gets under-priced.

In my previous post, using ANTAQ 2025 data, I looked at carrier-call concentration across the 10 main Brazilian container terminals. All 10 were at least moderately concentrated under DOJ/FTC 2010 HHI thresholds. None was unconcentrated.

Which means: choosing a port for fiscal reasons can also mean narrowing carrier optionality — without explicitly pricing that risk. Brazil's carrier structure can resemble, quietly, some of the port-dependency dynamics I saw in Angola and Mozambique.

This sharpens now. Brazil's 2023 tax reform (EC 132/2023) and Complementary Law 214/2025 phase down ICMS incentives from 2029, with full sunset by 2033. The fiscal layer is in transition; the carrier-structure layer is not.

A port can be fiscally attractive and still reduce carrier optionality. What you save in tax today, you may pay later in carrier dependency and weaker negotiation leverage.

The real procurement equation in Brazil:

Tax benefit + Inland cost + Service reliability + Carrier optionality = a real landed-cost decision.

Fiscal optimization is not wrong. It just needs to be weighed against the hidden cost of structural carrier dependency, on the same horizon.

For importers, freight forwarders, customs brokers and procurement teams: when you model landed cost in Brazil, do you include carrier optionality as a risk factor — or only freight, tax and inland cost?
